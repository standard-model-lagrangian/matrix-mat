ABSTRACT
The extracellular matrix (ECM) exhibits tissue-specific viscoelasticity with a unique combination of elasticity and viscosity.
Hydrogels with independently controlled elastic modulus and stress relaxation have been developed. Yet, time- and labor-efficient
identification of formulations used for the preparation of multicomponent hydrogels recapitulating the mechanical properties of
a specific tissue remains a challenge. Conventional bottom-up screening of hydrogel formulations is resource-intensive, especially
for navigating multicomponent hydrogels. Here, we present an active learning framework based on multi-objective Bayesian
optimization to accelerate the discovery of biomimetic fibrous hydrogels replicating the elastic and viscous properties of the ECM
of healthy and diseased tissues. In the data-driven approach, we iteratively navigated the multidimensional chemical space, while
simultaneously optimizing hydrogel’s conflicting mechanical properties and mapping the boundaries of material feasibility using
sparse experimental feedback. The experimental data were used to train a digital twin model and unveil the interplay between
the fabrication conditions and hydrogel properties. The decoupled effects of the elasticity and viscosity were established for
cell activation, proliferation, and morphogenesis. This work shows the potential of using the data-driven approach, leveraging
even sparse experimental data to engineer hydrogels and optimize diverse biomaterials for tissue engineering and regenerative
medicine.

Introduction

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a network of fibrous proteins,
proteoglycans, and polysaccharides that are secreted by cells and
provide structural support, mechanical cues, and biochemical sig-

nalling to regulate cellular behavior and functions [1–5]. The ECM
is viscoelastic [6], that is, it encompasses both elastic and viscous
characteristics. Elasticity, characterized by the elastic modulus,
reflects the ECM’s ability to restore its original shape after the
external force causing deformation is removed [7], while viscosity

The ECM of tissues in vivo has a distinct combination of the
elastic modulus and stress relaxation, which is characteristic of
each tissue and each particular physiological condition. Independent control of hydrogel elasticity and viscosity has been achieved
by varying the density of non-covalent or dynamic covalent
crosslinks [13–15], by using interpenetrating networks [16, 17],
or by modulating polymer chain entanglements [18]. However,
the recapitulation of tissue-specific mechanical properties relied
on a One-Factor-At-A-Time (OFAT) screening paradigm [19–
21], where individual hydrogel components and gel preparation
conditions were iteratively varied to achieve the targeted properties. This forward-design process becomes largely ineffective
for multicomponent hydrogels. As a result, OFAT often restricts
the exploration process to a narrow parametric window and
leaves most of the space of accessible properties unexplored. In
contrast, an effective bioengineering strategy relies on a reversedesign process that starts with the specific mechanophenotype
of a target tissue, followed by the identification of suitable
hydrogel preparation conditions. OFAT becomes inefficient in
such a reverse-design process [22] and therefore cannot efficiently
navigate in a multicomponent hydrogel fabrication space.
Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful paradigm
for accelerated materials discovery [23–26]. However, its application to biomaterials is hindered by the difficulty in collecting
large data sets [27]. More specifically, characterizing hydrogel
viscoelasticity and validating downstream cellular responses generally require labor-intensive manual experiments, which limit
data generation throughput. To facilitate the utility of ML in the
data-constrained regime, the design of experiments (DoE) was
employed [28–30]. However, the DoE-assisted ML is generally
confined to low-dimensional spaces with ≤3 inputs and fails to
capture correlations between conflicting properties. Moreover,
these models rely on static training sets, resulting in poor
generalizability and the inability to predict performance outside
the pre-defined experimental matrix. To overcome these limitations, active learning (AL) frameworks based on multi-objective
Bayesian optimization (MOBO) provided a data-efficient strategy
for material design [31]. Distinct from the DoE, AL operates as
a dynamic process that starts with specific design targets and
iteratively guides the experimental search toward the desired
properties [25, 32, 33]. This framework enables efficient reverse
design of biomimetic hydrogels, while simultaneously mapping
the boundaries of material feasibility. However, its application has
not yet been realized for designing hydrogels to recapitulate the
mechanophenotypes of native tissues.
Here, we report an AL workflow for accelerated reverse design
of fibrous hydrogels with tissue-mimetic viscoelastic properties.
By navigating the conflicting relationship between compres2 of 13

sive modulus and stress relaxation, this data-driven approach
guided the exploration of the multidimensional hydrogel design
space using sparse experimental feedback. We show that this
framework enabled effective search for the targeted combination
of viscoelastic properties of the gel and identified hydrogel
fabrication conditions recapitulating the mechanophenotypes of
healthy and scarred skin, as well as benign versus malignant
breast tumors. Rather than optimizing a single recipe, the model
simultaneously mapped the boundaries of material feasibility,
defining the accessible limits of the hydrogel system. By leveraging precisely controlled hydrogel properties, we successfully
decoupled the effects of matrix elasticity and viscosity on fibroblast activation, cancer cell proliferation, and cancer spheroid
morphogenesis. This work establishes a generalized framework
for the synthesis and fabrication of biomaterials with competing
mechanical properties, thus paving the way for the data-efficient
discovery of complex tissue models for regenerative medicine and
personalized disease modeling.

Results

2.1
Workflow of AL-Guided Hydrogel
Fabrication
A hydrogel was formed by (i) the assembly of aldehyde-modified
cellulose nanocrystals (a-CNCs) in the HBSS buffer, resulting
in a fibrous network where GelMA was colocalized with the
a-CNCs in the fibers, and (ii) covalent crosslinking of a-CNCs
and GelMA with the formation of imine crosslinking groups
(Figure 1a). In the hydrogel, GelMA contributed to the network
stability, introduced cell-adhesive ligands in the gel, and enabled
next-step photocrosslinking of the fibers using methacryloyl
groups. Intrafibrillar photocrosslinking of GelMA under ultraviolet (UV) irradiation using a photoinitiator, lithium phenyl-2,4,6trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP), provided additional control
of fiber stiffness that contributed to the elastic modulus of the
hydrogel [34]. In this work, GelMA with either low or high
content of methacryloyl groups (termed GelMA-L and GelMA-H,
respectively) was used.
Hydrogel fabrication was controlled by varying five parameters,
namely, (i) a-CNC concentration, (ii) GelMA-L concentration,
(iii) GelMA-H concentration, (iv) LAP concentration, and (v) the
UV-irradiation time. The parameter space for hydrogel fabrication is provided in Table S1. We estimate that a step change of
1.0 wt.% in the concentration of a-CNCs, GelMA-L, or GelMA-H,
0.05 wt.% in LAP concentration, and a 60s increment in the UV
exposure time would yield an experimental space of 6,230 possible
hydrogel fabrication conditions. Even to explore a fraction of such
a vast experimental space using an OFAT technique could take
at least 4–6 months for a highly trained scientist. Rather than
relying on the OFAT approach, we utilized an AL strategy for
the accelerated exploration of the 5D parameter space to fabricate
tissue-mimetic fibrous hydrogels with a targeted combination of
elastic modulus and stress relaxation.

2.2
Fabrication of Skin-Mimetic Hydrogel
Scaffolds
For the hydrogel mimicking the properties of the ECM of healthy
skin, we selected E = 1.5 ± 0.15 kPa and a σ100 = 25% ± 2.5% [35–
38]. Figure 2a shows the variation in the average E and σ100 values
of the hydrogels obtained in five experimental iterations. In each
iteration, four sets of distinct hydrogel fabrication conditions proposed by Atlas were marked as the numbers on the x-axis of the
graphs in Figure 2a. Gel fabrication conditions were annotated
using the iteration number, followed by the number of the set
of hydrogel fabrication conditions. For instance, the notation I1-2
corresponds to the first iteration and the second set of fabrication
conditions. The first three iterations did not result in the hydrogel
with targeted E and σ100 values. After the tolerances for E and
σ100 were reduced to zero to prioritize fabrication conditions
leading to the hydrogel’s properties with a minimal deviation
from the targets, two successful sets of fabrication conditions in
the fourth iteration, namely, I4-2 and I4-4 led to the hydrogels
with targeted values of both E and σ100 (Figure S2a,b and Table S3).
In the additional fifth iteration, the hydrogel’s properties did not
improve, indicating that Atlas identified the optimal fabrication
conditions within four iterations. Hence, the experimental search
was terminated. The hydrogels prepared under the identified
fabrication conditions had E = 1.41 ± 0.12 kPa and σ100 = 25%
± 1.0%, close to the values listed in Table 1. These hydrogels
were referred to as Healthy Skin I4-2 and Healthy Skin I4-4
hydrogels
Next, Atlas was instructed to search for the fabrication conditions
leading to a hydrogel with E = 12 ± 1.2 kPa and σ100 = 20% ± 2%,
which are characteristic of the ECM in scarred skin (Table 1) [39,
40]. Figure 2b shows the variation in the average E and σ100 values
of the gel produced in the Atlas-guided iterative experiments. In
the first four iterations, most hydrogels met the target for σ100
but failed to achieve the targeted value of E. When three more
experimental iterations were run with the UV-irradiation time

constrained below 60 s, the first fabrication condition achieving
the targeted E and σ100 , I6-4, was identified in the sixth iteration.
The hydrogels produced under I7-3 fabrication conditions also
satisfied the target values of both E and σ100 (Figure S2c,d
and Table S3). As no further improvement in these properties
was achieved in the eighth interaction, the experiments were
stopped. Two samples prepared under optimized conditions,
termed Scarred Skin I6-4 and Scarred Skin I7-3 hydrogels, had E
= 12.11 ± 1.03 kPa and σ100 = 22.5% ± 1.5%.
The structure of the Healthy Skin and Scarred Skin hydrogels
was examined by SEM imaging (Figure 2c). The hydrogels had a
fibrous structure characteristic of the native ECM in the skin [43,
44], with no statistical difference in fiber density for the samples
with similar mechanical properties (Figure 2d). The fiber density
of the Scarred Skin hydrogels was significantly higher than in the
Healthy Skin samples, which mimicked an increased density of
collagen fibers in the ECM of the scarred scar tissue [45, 46]. Thus,
the hydrogels recapitulated the difference in both the mechanical
properties and the structure of the ECM of healthy and scarred
tissues.
Since fibroblasts undergo prolonged activation into scar-making
contracting myofibroblasts during abnormal scarring [47], we
examined the effect of hydrogel viscoelasticity on fibroblast
activation. Human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were cultured on
1 mm-thick Healthy Skin and Scarred Skin hydrogel sheets for five
days with a 4-day treatment of profibrotic transforming growth
factor-β1 (TGF- β1). Figure 2e shows fibroblast morphology and
the expression of the myofibroblast marker, α-smooth muscle
actin (α-SMA), using immunofluorescence staining. The average
spreading area of HDFs on the Scarred Skin I6-4 and I7-3
hydrogels was significantly larger than on the Healthy Skin I42 and I4-4 samples (Figure 2f), reflecting the increased level
of intracellular stress [48]. This effect was further validated
by the expression of α-SMA [47]. For the cells cultured on
the Healthy Skin hydrogels, α-SMA was not incorporated into
stress fibers and remained as a soluble monomer (Figure 2ei,ii). In contrast, α-SMA assembled into actin filaments and was
colocalized with F-actin when HDFs were cultured on the Scarred
Skin hydrogel (Figure 2e-iii,iv). The co-localization of α-SMA
and F-actin was quantified by the Pearson correlation coefficient
(PCC) (Figure 2g). The PCC values of 0.60 and 0.45 for the
cells seeded on the Scarred Skin and Healthy Skin hydrogels,
respectively, indicated a stronger colocalization of α-SMA and
F-actin in the HDFs cultured on the Scarred Skin hydrogels.
Thus, fibroblasts cultured in the Scarred Skin gel recapitulated
features of fibroblast activation associated with pathological
scarring.

Since tissue-mimetic models enhance the ability to elucidate,
test, and validate the role of the mechanical properties of the
ECM in cancer development and drug response [49–51], we
focused on the fabrication of hydrogels representing the ECM
of benign and malignant tumors. We first searched for the
fabrication conditions of the hydrogels with E = 1.3 ± 0.13 kPa
and σ100 = 50% ± 5% (Table 1), which recapitulated soft and
strongly relaxing benign tumor ECM [42, 52]. Figure 3a shows
the variation in the average E and σ100 values of the hydrogel
fabricated in seven closed-loop experimental series. In the third
and fourth iterations, two sets of fabrication conditions, namely,
I3-3 and I4-1, led to the gels with the target E and σ100 values
(Figure S3 and Table S4). Notably, these two conditions were
identified without human intervention during the data-driven
optimization and experiment-selection steps. The experiments
were completed after the seventh iteration, as no further improvement was observed in the fifth and sixth iterations, and results
diverged from target values in the seventh iteration. Therefore,
the optimal conditions were identified within four iterations.
The hydrogels fabricated under optimal I3-3 and I4-1 conditions
had E = 1.40 ± 0.12 kPa and a σ100 = 47.5% ± 3.5%, which
were termed as Soft Tumor I3-3 and Soft Tumor I4-1 hydrogels,
respectively.
In the next experimental campaign, we utilized the data-driven
strategy to discover the conditions for the fabrication of the
hydrogel mimicking stiff tumor ECM with E = 16 kPa. Given
a broad range of σ100 values reported in the literature for such
ECM, the primary objective was set to maximize the value of σ100 ,
while achieving E = 16 kPa. As shown in Figure 3b, in the first
iteration, conditions I1-1 and I1-4 met the target value of E, with
I1-4 providing σ100 = 12%. In the subsequent four iterations, all
fabrication conditions showed no improvement in the hydrogel’s
mechanical properties. The sample with E = 16.8 kPa and σ100
= 12.0% (fabrication condition I1-4, Figure S3 and Table S4) was
selected as a Stiff Tumor I1-4 hydrogel.
Figure 3c shows the structure of Soft Tumor and Stiff Tumor
hydrogels prepared using the AL-proposed fabrication conditions. The Soft Tumor I3-1 and I4-1 hydrogels had a fibrous structure (Figure 3c-i,ii), which was less evident for the Stiff Tumor
hydrogel I1-4 (Figure 3c-iii) due to the inter-fiber crosslinking and
a higher fiber density (Figure 3d).
The Soft Tumor and Stiff Tumor hydrogels were used as scaffolds
for the growth of spheroids from the human breast cancer cell
line MCF-7. After a 15-day culture, spheroid morphology varied
depending on the hydrogel’s mechanical properties (Figure 3e).
Spheroids grown in the Soft Tumor I3-3 and I4-1 hydrogels were

larger and had a more irregular shape (Figure 3e-i,ii; Figure S5a,b)
than spheroids formed in the Stiff Tumor I1-4 hydrogel (Figure 3eiii; Figure S5c). To characterize spheroid shapes quantitatively,
their circularity was calculated as the area-to-perimeter ratio (see
Methods). In the Soft Tumor hydrogels, the circularity decreased
over time (Figure 3f), indicating that spheroids gradually adopted
an irregular shape. In contrast, spheroids growing in the Stiff
Tumor hydrogel retained their circularity of approximately 0.87
from Day 5 to Day 15. In addition, Figure 3g shows that
on day 15, spheroids grown in the Soft Tumor I3-1 and I4-1
hydrogels had a significantly larger area than spheroids grown
in the Stiff Tumor I1-2 hydrogel. To decouple the effects of
matrix biomechanics and GelMA concentration, we fabricated
an additional soft tumor hydrogel, Soft Tumor (concentrationconstrained) hydrogel, with viscoelastic properties similar to
those of the Soft Tumor hydrogels, but with GelMA concentration
matching that of the Stiff Tumor hydrogel (Figure S4). As shown
in Figure S6, on day 15, the spheroids formed in the Soft Tumor
(concentration-constrained) hydrogel gained a size comparable
to that of the spheroids cultured in the Soft Tumor hydrogels
but had an approximately 10% higher circularity. These results
indicate that spheroid growth was primarily governed by the
matrix viscoelasticity, whereas the GelMA concentration affected
spheroid morphology.
To decouple the effect of elastic modulus and stress relaxation
on tumor spheroid growth, we compared spheroid growth in two
hydrogels with similar elasticity but different relaxation behavior,
that is, in the Soft Tumor I4-1 gel and the Healthy Skin I4-2 gel. As
shown in Figure S8 and Table S5, these two hydrogels exhibited
comparable elastic moduli (1.44 vs. 1.43 kPa, respectively), but
substantially different degrees of relaxation (46.5 vs. 25%), relaxation times (43.8 vs. 164 s in the slow relaxation timescale, τ2 ), and
relaxation half-time (119 vs.548 s). The spheroids cultured in the
Healthy Skin I4-2 hydrogel maintained a similar average area but
had a more spherical shape (Figure S7), compared to spheroids
grown in the Soft Tumor hydrogels. This observation indicated
that the degree of stress relaxation of the hydrogel mostly affected
spheroid shape, in agreement with previous reports on the effect
of matrix viscosity on the morphogenesis of tumor spheroids
[19, 21]. To examine the role of gel elasticity, we compared
spheroids grown in the Stiff Tumor I1-2 hydrogel (E = 16.8 kPa,
σ100 = 12.0%) and Healthy Skin I4-2 hydrogel (E = 1.44 kPa,
σ100 = 25%). In the stiffer hydrogel, on Day 15, spheroids were
significantly smaller but exhibited a similar circularity as in the
soft hydrogel, which indicated that hydrogel elasticity primarily
affects spheroid size. Collectively, these results reflect a specific
role of each mechanical property: enhanced stress relaxation
resulted in the irregular spheroid shape, due to enhanced cell
migration in a rapidly relaxing matrix [42]; increased gel stiffness
restricted spheroid growth by inducing cell cycle arrest through
compressive stress [53]. Overall, these findings underline the

2.4
Pareto-Front Mapping of the Tissue-Mimetic
Fibrous Hydrogels
The challenge in the fabrication of hydrogels with high elastic
modulus and strong relaxation stems from the competition
between these properties. We utilized Atlas to map the Pareto
front for the hydrogels fabricated under different conditions to
find the combination of hydrogel properties for which any further
increase in the E value beyond the Pareto front would require a
compromise in σ100 [54]. Delineating the trade-off relationship
between elasticity and viscosity allowed us to (i) validate the
identified conditions for the fabrication of stiff tumor hydrogels
with the strongest possible relaxation and (ii) explore a feasible
combination of the E and σ100 values.
To rapidly identify the Pareto front of E and σ100 for a given hydrogel, we used hypervolume (HV) as the performance metric that
measured the enclosed space under the E and σ100 curve of the
highest-performing hydrogels [55]. A higher HV value represents
a broader range of achievable combinations of hydrogels’ elasticity and viscosity. To initiate the Pareto-front mapping campaign,
we utilized 100 data points generated in the previous closed-loop
experiments as the training dataset of the surrogate model. Next,
this pre-trained surrogate model of Atlas was utilized to expand
the boundary of E and σ100 Pareto front. The iterative mapping
process continued until the HV value reached a plateau. Figure 4a
shows an increase in the HV value throughout this iterative
Pareto-front mapping process, which was initially guided by a
utopian target strategy and subsequently, by the HV improvement

2.5

Analysis of Hydrogel Fabrication Conditions

The extensive experimental data collected during the fabrication
of the tissue-mimetic hydrogels and Pareto-front mapping provided the ability to create a model of the hydrogel properties,
termed “digital twin” [56], which predicts the value of E or σ100 of
the hydrogel, based on the specific set of fabrication conditions.
The 168 sets of experimental results (that is, the fabrication
conditions and the corresponding E and σ100 values) were used
to train the digital twin of the hydrogels to enable the prediction
of E and σ100 for a given set of fabrication conditions. After testing
and fine-tuning various ML algorithms, the digital twin based
on gradient boosting showed the highest accuracy in predicting
both E and σ100 (Table S6). Next, the optimal digital twin was
used to study the impact of a specific fabrication parameter on the
hydrogel E and σ100 using Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)
analysis (Figure 5) [57]. A higher positive SHAP value indicates
a stronger positive relationship between the parameter and the
hydrogel property, whereas a negative SHAP value indicates an
inverse relationship.
In Figure 5a,b (top-to-bottom), the concentrations of GelMAL, GelMA-H, a-CNC, and LAP and the UV-exposure time are
represented on the y-axis in the order of the decreasing impact
on each mechanical property of the hydrogel. Each dot in this
figure represents a specific fabrication parameter, with blue and
red colors indicating low and high reagent concentrations or
exposure time, respectively, with respect to the mean of the
corresponding parameter. The distance of each dot from the
vertical line reflects its SHAP value.
An increase in the concentrations of GelMA-H, a-CNCs, GelMAL, and LAP and the UV exposure time led to higher hydrogel
elasticity (Figure 5a). The concentration of GelMA-H had the
most significant effect by affecting intrafibrillar crosslinking.
The a-CNC concentration had the second strongest effect, as
it determined fiber density in the hydrogel. The concentration
of LAP was of the least importance, which indicated that the
degree of intrafibrillar crosslinking was primarily governed by
the concentrations of GelMA-H and GelMA-L. Figure 5b shows
the effect of the hydrogel fabrication parameters on the value of
σ100 . The concentration of GelMA-H had the greatest negative
impact on σ100 , due to the covalent crosslinks formed within
the hydrogel fibers. Interestingly, the relationship between the aCNC concentration and σ100 was not linear. While at high a-CNC
concentrations, the value of σ100 was reduced, increasing a-CNC
Advanced Functional Materials, 2026

concentration in the low-to-moderate range mildly enhanced
stress relaxation. Such an improvement can be attributed to the
physical crosslinking of a-CNCs [58] or the dynamic nature of
the imine crosslinks between a-CNCs and GelMA [59], both
of which contribute to stress dissipation. However, at higher
a-CNC concentrations, the increased fiber density introduced
more extensive entanglement, which restricted fiber mobility and
hindered the ability of the hydrogel network to relax stress.

3

Discussion

The challenge in the reverse design of tissue-mimetic hydrogels
with targeted properties lies in navigating complex multicomponent formulations, while simultaneously resolving intrinsically
conflicting properties. In this study, the theoretical design space
for the multicomponent composite fibrous hydrogel encompassed over 6230 possible fabrication conditions. Conventional
OFAT screening becomes inefficient in navigating this vast
multidimensional landscape. While elastic modulus and stress
relaxation are often inversely coupled in hydrogels, OFAT is
incapable of mapping complex property interactions and therefore becomes ineffective and even fails in target identification.
By employing a hierarchical MOBO, we demonstrate that the
AL framework effectively identifies optimal hydrogel fabrication
conditions with only a small number of experiments. This strategy underscores the advantages of AL for the rational biomaterial
design, particularly when resolving intrinsic property conflicts.
An important insight provided by the MOBO algorithm was the
rigorous delineation of the Pareto front of the viscoelasticity of
the hydrogels. The Pareto front of the gels was obtained using 100
conditions collected from the closed-loop experiments, with 68
additional experiments designated for Pareto front mapping. The
algorithm maintained a strategic balance between exploitation
toward the optimization target and exploration to map out
the hydrogel viscoelastic landscape. In contrast, identifying the
Pareto front by using OFAT or conventional DoE-assisted ML
is highly challenging and usually requires extensive tests across
all possible parameter combinations. The Pareto-front mapping
outlined the competing relationship between E and σ100 characteristics. The results of the SHAP analysis uncovered the level
of importance of different fabrication conditions on the elastic
and viscous properties of the hydrogels. While achieving high
elastic modulus and strong relaxation simultaneously remained
a challenging task due to the covalent bonding within GelMA,

In the simulated comparison of Atlas with random search and

framework succeeded in its primary objective of identifying
hydrogel fabrication conditions for specific mechanotypes with
minimal resource expenditure. Such robustness is attributed
to the Gaussian Process (GP) regression embedded within the
Atlas algorithm. Unlike neural networks that require massive
datasets to converge, GPs are probabilistic models designed to
maximize likelihood in low-data regimes (<100 data points)
through built-in regularization [60–62]. Moreover, the resulting
“digital twin” models demonstrated strong generalization to
untested parameters within the parameter space, thus ensuring
that rapid optimization was a result of efficient search dynamics,
rather than memorization or overfitting.

4

Conclusion

We demonstrated an AL framework that enabled data-efficient
reverse design of fibrous hydrogels with targeted tissue-mimetic
viscoelasticity. By employing MOBO to navigate the intrinsic
trade-offs between the gel elastic modulus and stress relaxation,
this framework enabled the rapid identification of optimal fabrication conditions of hydrogels recapitulating the biomechanics
of the ECM of healthy and fibrotic skin, as well as benign
and malignant breast tumors. Beyond accelerating the discovery,
the algorithm successfully mapped the boundaries of material
feasibility. The resulting mechanomimetic scaffolds enabled the
growth of fibroblast and tumor cells in a microenvironment with
biomimetic mechanical properties. The developed data-driven
approach established a platform for future mechanistic investigation of the role of viscoelasticity of the microenvironment in
biological processes. The AL-guided strategy developed in this
work highlights a data-driven approach to the acceleration of
material discovery and the translation from lab-designed materials to patient-specific tissue mimetics to approach personalized
medicine from a material perspective.

to maximum at 1010 . To maximize the HV, the target values for

each iteration, the ML algorithm was asked to generate 4 sets of
fabrication conditions (that is, the batch size was 4). If 4 sets of
fabrication conditions were practically similar, the batch size was
increased to generate 4 distinct sets of fabrication conditions.

Hydrogel Preparation and Characterization

Imaging Hydrogel Structure

The hydrogel samples were prepared using supercritical CO2

were fractured and processed in an Autosamdri-810 Tousimis
critical point dryer to replace the ethanol with liquid CO2 , which
was then removed by slow venting at the supercritical state.
The dried hydrogels were gold-coated using an SC7640 HighResolution Sputter Coater (Quorum Technologies) and imaged
with a Quanta FEI scanning electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Fiber density and diameter were determined
using the ImageJ plugin DiameterJ (https://imagej.net/plugins/
diameterj).

5.4

Cell Culture

The hydrogels were gelled in the 35 mm-diameter confocal dish
with a glass bottom diameter of 20 mm (VWR, 75856-746). Primary
neonatal human dermal fibroblasts (ATCC, PCS-201-010) were
seeded on crosslinked skin ECM-mimetic hydrogels at a density
of 10 cells/µL in 1 mL complete DMEM culture medium supplemented 10 vol.% FBS, and 1.0 vol.% penicillin/streptomycin.
On day 2, the fibroblasts were exposed for 4 days to DMEM
containing 2 ng mL−1 recombinant active TGF-𝛽1, 2 vol.% FBS,
and 1.0 vol.% penicillin/streptomycin. For hydrogels mimicking
breast tumor ECM, the human breast adenocarcinoma cell line,
MCF-7 (ATCC, HTB-22) was suspended in the hydrogel precursor
mixture at a density of 100 cells/µL. The cell-laden hydrogels
were crosslinked as described above. After gelation, 1 mL of
complete DMEM containing 10 vol.% FBS and 1.0 vol.% penicillin/streptomycin was added to the dish, and the encapsulated
cells were cultured for 15 days with medium change every 5
days.

another 60 min incubation with secondary antibodies. Between
each step, the samples were washed three times using 1X
